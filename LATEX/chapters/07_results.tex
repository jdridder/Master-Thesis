\chapter{Results on Surrogate Model Performance}
For the successful application of physics-constrained (PC) surrogate models
under uncertainty in MPC, several performance indicators are of major interest.
These include the computational efficiency during the offline training phase,
the degree of physical balance violation and the adequacy of the uncertainty quantification.
Finally, the suitability of the derived NARX models in an MPC algorithm should be evaluated.


\section{Training Efficiency}
\label{results:sec:training-efficiency}

To assess, how the PC activation function alters the training progress,
the \emph{validation loss} is analysed. For simplicity, one NARX predictor is trained in two different
variations and varying number of hidden neurons using the
MSE. The first one is configured as a basic NN (vanilla). The second one holds the same configuration
and additionally applies the PC activation function after its last layer (PC).
The training data is generated using constant model parameters. Thus, no uncertainty distribution
is present. PC-NARX (solid line) and vanilla-NARX models (dashed line) are compared
during their evolvement over $512$ training epochs (fig.~\ref{results:fig:validation_loss}).
As shallow neural networks, all NARX models posses only \emph{one} hidden layer with either
$64$ (purple), $16$ (blue) or $4$ (yellow) hidden neurons. For a non-linear reaction system, that includes 24 outputs, the low amount
of $4$ hidden neurons represents a challenge. The training is performed with identical learning rates
of \SI{5e-5} and batch sizes of $1024$ for all three NARX variations using \SI{1.024e6}{} data points.
Here, the training and validation fractions are $0.8$ and $0.2$ respectively.
\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Figures/Results/001_val_loss.pdf}
    \caption[Validation loss of vanilla NARX vs PC-NARX]{The validation loss calculated as MSE $\mathcal{L}_\mathrm{MSE, val}$
    during the training of the vanilla NARX (dashed line) and the PC-NARX (solid line). The three data rows indicate
    \emph{one hidden layer} with $64, 16$ and $4$ hidden neurons each. The PC-NARX converges faster
    than the vanilla NARX.}
    \label{results:fig:validation_loss}
\end{figure}
\newline
\newline
All PC-NARX models with varying number of hidden neurons
converge faster to lower validation losses than
their unconstrained peers. After two consecutive drops in validation loss during the first $150$ epochs,
all models stagnate which is common in NN training. Close to a local minimum w.r.t to the trainable
parameters, only slow improvement is made. Changes in the loss function become rather 
stochastic in \emph{stochastic gradient descent} (SGD) than systematic, when the gradient gets close to zero.
Without regard to the training phase, the PC-NARX models always outperform the vanilla-NARX models
with lower validation losses. The validation loss suggests that at $512$ epochs, a PC-NARX with $16$ hidden 
neurons (solid, blue) is equally as accurate as a vanilla-NARX with $64$ hidden neurons (dashed, purple) (fig.~\ref{results:fig:validation_loss}).
Similarly, the PC-NARX with $4$ hidden neurons presents a validation loss close to the vanilla-NARX with
$16$ hidden neurons. This implies, the network size can be reduced to a quarter while achieving
the same validation loss.
\newline
\newline
It is questionable however, whether the PC-NARX can maintain its advantage for a growing number of training epochs.
As the balance constraint implemented into the PC-NARX is present in the data itself, the vanilla-NARX
will approximate it on the domain of the data up to validation accuracy,
if the number of epochs is sufficiently high.
The minimum possible 
validation loss is determined by the network size, amount and quality of training data and training epochs.
So theoretically, the vanilla-NARX should approach the PC-NARX in excessive neural network training,
if a sufficiently large amount of high-quality data is supplied.
\newline
\newline
In reality, training data is often noisy and only available in limited amount.
Computing power and memory are expensive in time and investment. The training duration
and network complexity should be kept to a minimum. Hence, the PC activation
presents a great opportunity to speed up NN training convergence. It also
allows to reduce the network size or the amount of training data,
while the prediction accuracy of an unconstrained NN is retained. It can be shown, that
PCNNs perform \SI{18}{\%} better than basic NNs when the amount training
data is reduced to \SI{60}{\%} in one specific case of modelling an extractive distillation~\cite{chen2024}.
For a process model in the simulation software \emph{Aspen Plus}, the PC-NN yielded an improvement of \SI{25}{\%} w.r.t validation loss 
under the same conditions. These findings by Chen et al. \cite{chen2024} correlate with this work.
Reducing the network size is especially interesting for MPC applications, where it
significantly influences the online optimization w.r.t the input variables.
Thus, reducing the network size of a PC-NARX may be truly advantagous.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
The facilitated validation convergence implies that not only the direct NN output is correct
but its gradient as well. In SGD, the gradient exhibits noise, because only the points are
considered by the gradient, that are part of the current batch. It is reasonable, that
the correction by the physics-constraints reduces the stochasticity of the gradient
in each iteration in SGD an therefore allows faster convergence. 
The effect on the gradient can be investigated by looking at the first partial derivative.
The first step of backpropagation may be given with
the loss function $\mathcal{L}$ and the network parameters $\bm{\theta}_i$ 
(eq.~\ref{results:eq:backpropagation-first-step}) \cite{deisenroth2019}.
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:backpropagation-first-step}
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(\vy^*)}{\partial \bm{\theta}_i} = 
    \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}(\vy^*)}{\partial \vy^*} 
    \frac{\partial g_\text{pc}}{\partial \bm{\tilde{y}}}
    \frac{\partial \bm{\tilde{y}}}{\partial \bm{\theta}_i} 
\end{equation}

\noindent
The partial derivative of $g_\text{pc}$ connects the gradient of the plain NN
$\partial \bm{\tilde{y}} / \partial \bm{\theta}_i$ 
to the overall gradient of the loss function. It can be computed directly
from the definition of the activation function (eq.~\ref{results:eq:pc-effect-on-backpropagation}
and cor.~\ref{cor: physics constrained solution}).

\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:pc-effect-on-backpropagation}
    \frac{\partial g_\text{pc}}{\partial \bm{\tilde{y}}} =
    \frac{\partial}{\partial \bm{\tilde{y}}} \begin{pmatrix} \bm{K}
        \begin{pmatrix}
            2 \bm{\tilde{y}} \\
            \bm{A} \vz_0 + \vb
        \end{pmatrix}
    \end{pmatrix} =
    2 \bm{K}_{:,:n_\text{out}}
\end{equation}
\noindent
Thus, the gradient of the NN is modified using a linear transformation with a submatrix of
the projection inverse $\bm{K}$. This is the solution inverse of the KKT-conditions of the original
(QP) (def.~\ref{th: physics constrained KKT}) and acts as a projection of the output into the feasible space,
that is defined by the program.
The same is done to the gradient without taking the reference point
into account enforcing only feasible directions.
Consequently, this shrinks 
the noisiness of the resulting network parameters and network output. This may also become
an advantage for the application in MPC, where network gradients w.r.t the 
control inputs instead of the trainable parameters are calculated.
The direct modification of the gradient underlines that the conditioning of $\bm{K}$
is of great importance to ensure numerical stability during NN training.
Ill-conditioned programs, perhaps with unscaled constraints, will certainly lead to numerical issues.

\section{Physics-Consistency}

To analyze the physics-consistency of the different NARX frameworks, training is performed using 
data generated under parametric uncertainty. All NNs are configured equally using the displayed
hyperparameters in \texttt{pyTorch} (tab.~\ref{results:tab:NARX-configuration}).
The dimension of the input vector is chosen to equal $16$ to achieve a reasonable balance between
PCA compression and data representation. All models consist of one hidden layer with $32$ neurons.

    }
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{cccccc}
        \toprule
        input size & hidden neurons & learning rate & training ratio & $\sigma_\text{w}$ & $\alpha$ \\
        \midrule
        16 & [32] & \SI{5e-5}{} & 0.8 & 0.001 & 0.2 \\
    \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \label{results:tab:NARX-configuration}
\end{table}
\newline
\newline
\noindent
The training is performed with a constant learning rate of \SI{5e-5}{} and a training to validation ratio of $0.8:0.2$.
Furthermore, the significance level $\alpha$ of the uncertainty quantification is set to $0.2$ which
corresponds to an upper quantile model of $0.9$ and a lower quantile model of $0.1$.
Regarding the weight function for the quantile conditioning (eq.~\ref{sur:def: gaussian weight function}),
the weight parameter $\sigma_\text{w}$ is set to $0.001$ to emphasize data in close proximity to the
quantile predictions. During training, early-stopping is employed based on the validation loss to avoid overfitting 
and redundant training durations. Training is executed once for the vanilla and once for the PC-NARX
framework.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
As a next step, an open-loop simulation is performed in \texttt{do-mpc}.
It propagates the measurement trajectories for the nominal case, the upper and the lower quantiles forward
in time independently (def.~\ref{sur:def: general rhs for uncertainty quantification}). This is done for 
the three framework variations \emph{vanilla}, \emph{naive} and \emph{pc}. Further, the simulation
horizon extends to \SI{480}{\second} which portrays challenging conditions in contrast to the intended MPC
prediction horizon of \SI{30}{\second}. The main 
performance metric is the balance violation of the conservation of atoms due to stoichiometry (th.~\ref{case:cor: conservation of atoms}).
When it is equal to the zero vector, it is perfectly fulfilled.
The residual of the linear balance constraint is calculated (eq.~\ref{results:eq:residual of balance equation})
as the $l_2$-norm
and plotted as a function of time (fig.~\ref{results:fig:pc_violation_vanilla}).
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:residual of balance equation}
    ||\bm{b}||_2 = ||\bm{B} \bm{\Delta \xi}||_2
\end{equation}

    of the vanilla NARX framework. The nominal trajectory $\mathbb{E}$ (green) displays slightly better alignment
    with the balance than the models conditioned by quantiles $Q_{0.9}$ and $Q_{0.1}$.}
    \label{results:fig:pc_violation_vanilla}
\end{figure}
\noindent
To indicate maximum machine precision, horizontal dashed lines are drawn for \SI{64}{\bit} (gray)
and \SI{32}{\bit} (black). The balance residual norm $||\vb||_2$ is shown for the nominal NARX (green), trained
with a standard MSE, and the conditioned NARX models, that are trained with a weighted MSE.
Here, yellow colors the NARX conditioned by the $0.9$-quantile and purple the NARX conditioned by the $0.1$-quantile.
After beeing initialized with an initial measurement, that obeys the balance up to an error of \SI{1e-14}{}, 
all NARX models abruptly increase the error to \SI{1e-3}{}. They fluctuate in this region until the simulation
horizon is reached. When the residual is averaged over the simulation horizon using the $\langle(\cdot)\rangle_t$-
notation, the nominal NARX model produces a slightly smaller error of \SI{6.39e-4}{}.
In contrast, the error of the quantile-conditioned NARX models is roughly twice as big with values
of \SI{1.12e-3} and \SI{1.42e-3} for the $0.9$ and $0.1$ quantile respectively (fig.~\ref{results:fig:pc_violation_vanilla}).
It can be noticed that neither an instability, nor a divergence occurs,
that would lead to a drastic increase in the residual error.
Additionally, the error does not add up over time.

\end{figure}

\newline
The same is done with the naive-NARX models that are not trained with the PC
activation, but simply apply it to their output (fig.~\ref{results:fig:pc_violation_naive}). The 
balance error during the simulation shows a completely different picture. All models achieve
vanishing balance errors at \SI{64}{bit} precision of \SI{3.4e-16}{}. These appear consistent over the complete
simulation horizon. The naively PC models exhibit even smaller balance errors than the initial measurement itself, which stems
from a first principle simulation, due to fewer rounding errors. There is no noticable difference
between the nominal predictions $\mathbb{E}$ (green) and the conditioned quantile predictions $Q_{0.9}$ (yellow) and $Q_{0.1}$ (purple)
in contrast to their vanilla versions. Additionally, this can be interpreted as a proof of concept
for the PC activation $g_\text{pc}$. It enforces linear equality constraints up to machine precision, even
if it is not used during training.

    of the vanilla NARX framework.}

\end{figure}
\newline
\newline
Finally, the balance error is plotted for the PC-NARX, that uses the PC
activation during training and simulation (fig.~\ref{results:fig:pc_violation_pc}). While it significantly improves
training efficiency, it also enforces linear equality constraints in an open-loop simulation up to \SI{32}{\bit} precision.
Similarly to the naive application, all three NARX models achieve the same contraint residual error
of \SI{1.7e-7}{}. A difference between the PC nominal trajectory (green) and the quantile conditioned (yellow, purple)
trajectories is not existent. It is worth noticing, that the minimum possible contraint residual differs 
between the naive- and the PC-NARX. The naive version is not trained with the 
PC activation $g_\text{pc}$. It is applied \emph{a posteriori} and implemented using
a \texttt{casadi}-function object. The PC version owns a direct implementation of $g_\text{pc}$
into a \texttt{torch.nn.Module}. Eventhough it is converted into a \texttt{casadi} expression to be
simulated in \texttt{do-mpc}, it still keeps the \SI{32}{\bit} precision of the \texttt{torch.float32}-object.
Because the \texttt{casadi}-function computes in the \SI{64}{\bit} decimal space, naive- and PC-NARX
differ in maximum machine precision. Since the residual norm shrinks to insignificance
with values at \SI{1e-7} or \SI{1e-16} in both cases, \SI{32}{\bit} precision is not considered
disadvantageous. If truly needed, \SI{64}{\bit} tensor objects can be adapted easily.

\label{results:sec:simulation-capability}

To investigate, whether the NARX framework is able to track the distribution of measurement trajectories, 
the simulation results for the expected value and quantile NARX models are illustrated (fig.~\ref{results:fig:vanilla_temperature_sim}).

\newline
\newline
First, a look at the temperature is taken, which undergoes quantile regression and measurement propagation via

the first quarter $z=0.25L$, where a hotspot is likely to form. While yellow, green and purple

test trajectories are marked in light blue. These originate from first principle model simulations
with MC-sampled parameter combinations along the normal distributions. By their transparent blue
color, a trajectory density distribution becomes visible with darker blue at the trajectory mean

residence time of its fluid elements is smaller. Consequently, trajectories differing in parameters have less time to drift apart.
\newline
\newline
The vanilla expected value for the temperature (green) tracks the mean of the system reasonably well
at both positions, since its distance to the darkest part of the density distribition is mostly small.
The lower quantile bound (purple) is located below the mean prediction at all times and positions.
It also portrays a lower boundary for the measurement distribution as intended.
The same yields for the
upper quantile (yellow), which should cover a fraction of $0.9$ of temperature trajectories. 
However, the quantile models successfully quantify the uncertainty difference between reactor positions
with narrow intervals at $z=0.25L$ and wider intervals at $z=L$. Smaller intervals seem to be more
challenging to predict, as the $0.9$-quantile NARX excludes the mean prediction at \SI{120}{\second} and
$z=0.25L$. Crucially, the plotted quantile trajectories are simulated not only via recursive quantile temperature predictions 
but also via the conditioned NARX for the dimensionless concentration measure (fig.~\ref{case:fig: narx framework}).
This may deteriorate the quantile prediction in contrast to the expected value NARX, which relies
completely on least square regression. The according trajectories of $\bm{\chi}$ 
for the concentration measure for ethylene oxide at the same reactor

Here, the expected value NARX (green) $\mathbb{E}$ again follows the trajectory mean sufficiently.
The conditioned quantile trajectories (yellow and purple) however, cannot be accounted for

Especially, for the position $z=0.25L$ with a tight uncertainty distribition, the

a rather pragmatic approach to allow for independent propagation of temperature quantiles.

\section{Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{results:sec:uncertain-quantification}

The quality of the uncertainty quantification (UQ) is evaluated with two key measures – \emph{coverage} and 
\emph{relative interval width}. Coverage resembels the key idea behind quantiles, where
each quantile \emph{covers} a fraction of $\tau$ of the values within a distribution. The real 0.9-quantile
covers \SI{90}{\%} of values, the real 0.1-quantile covers \SI{10}{\%} of values, whereas the interval
between covers \SI{80}{\%} of the values. So to calculate the coverage of a given predicted interval
for an output $j$, the fraction of ground truth points must be determined that lie within (eq.~\ref{results:eq: coverage}) \cite{kemmerling2025}.
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq: coverage}
    \mathrm{coverage}_j = \frac{1}{m} \sum^m_{i=1} 
    \bm{1}_{y_{j,i} \in [\tilde{y}^\downarrow_{j,i} \;, \tilde{y}^\uparrow_{j,i}]}
\end{equation}
Here, $\bm{1}$ represents the indicator function, that counts the number of instances, in which the indexed 
condition is fulfilled. $m$ marks the number of total points, $y_{j,i}$ the true data point and $\tilde{y}_{j,i}^{(\cdot)}$
the respective interval predictions. The closer the coverage approaches the specified coverage during
quantile regression training, the more reliable the model quantifies the uncertainty.
It can be seen as the accuracy of the quantile models.
\newline
\newline
As the second measure, the relative interval width describes the precision of the uncertainty quantification.
A model delivering the desired coverage with a tight interval is called precise in its prediction, whereas 
wide intervals mean less precision. Coverage can be seen as an absolute measure for the quality of a
quantile prediction, whereas the relative interval width compares the efficiency
of models with equal coverages.
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq: relative interval width}
    \mathrm{rel. \; intervall \; width}_j = \frac{1}{m} \sum^m_{i=1} \frac{\tilde{y}^\uparrow_{j,i} - \tilde{y}^\downarrow_{j,i}}{|y_{j,i}|}
\end{equation}
\newline
These two measures are compared between the three NARX frameworks vanilla, naive and PC in the open-loop
simulation environment seen previously.
Mentioned frameworks are simulated forward \SI{512} time steps for 100 independent input trajectories
with a step size of \SI{1}{\second}.
For each trajectory, the first principle model is simulated accordingly $200$ times with MC-sampled
parameter combinations to calculate the interval coverages. The yielded coverages (purple) and relative intervall
widths (green) are supplied via boxplots for the vanilla framework (fig.~\ref{results:fig:vanilla-coverage-width}).
Additionally, the results are listed in terms of mean and standard deviation (tab.~\ref{results:tab: coverage_intervall_width}).

    {Coverage (purple) and relative interval width (green) for the temperature quantiles of the NARX models in the vanilla framework
    grouped by measurement position. The ideal intended coverage (dashed line) lies at $0.8$.}
    \label{results:fig:vanilla-coverage-width}

\newline
\newline
The mentioned metrics are determined for all four measurement positions (sec.~\ref{case:measurement-positions})
and shown in a twin plot with the coverage on the left (purple) and the relative interval width (green) on the
right y-axis. The ideal coverage defined in training is marked at $0.8$ (gray, dashed) line. Perfect 
quantile regressors would hit this target. Noticably, the vanilla framework
achieves perfect coverage by its median at the first measurement position with a range from $0.4$ to $1.0$.
Its mean coverage increases successively from \SI{0.814}{} towards extreme over-coverage of
\SI{0.983}{} over the reactor length, whereas its lowest occuring value dreases from $0.4$ to $0.95$
at the reactor outlet. The framework failes to provide the desired coverage of $0.8$ at all measurement
positions. Still, regarding temperature as a maximum constraint, over-coverage is preferred over
under-coverage, as the former promotes robust constraint satisfaction at the cost of greater conservativeness.
\newline
\newline
As the precision measure, the relative interval width (green) increases almost linearly from
\SI{6.51e-3}{} to \SI{2.17e-2}{} over the spatial coordinate with a symmetric distribution (fig.~\ref{results:fig:vanilla-coverage-width}).
With values of up to \SI{2.23}{\%} of the expected temperature prediction, the temperature distribution
itself can be seen as tight (tab.~\ref{results:tab: coverage_intervall_width}).
This can be checked by inspecting the temperature trajectories of the forward simulation (fig.~\ref{results:fig:vanilla_temperature_sim}).
Further, the increase is expected and mimics the higher uncertainty in the temperature caused by different parameters
due to the longer residence time. This tendency is a direct result of non-dispersive plug-flow behavior.
However, the magnitude of increase in relative interval width occurs in such a way, that over-coverage
is achieved. The confidence intervals are wider than necessary leading to higher conservativeness inspite
of quantile calibration with CQR (sec.~\ref{theory: CQR}) and a more complex network structure.

followed by an over-coverage in spatially increasing manner. Especially the rising uncertainty
along the axial reactor coordinate due to succeeding residence time represents a challenge
for recursive quantile estimation. Even enhancements such as CQR and a PC
activation function cannot provide a remedy. The models struggle to capture the heteroscedasticity in 
the distribution caused by the spatial coordinate. It may be beneficial to introduce
the measurement position as an NN input. This could help learning the spatial dependence better as well as
reducing the output size.
It is important to note that the desired coverage is only learned for the one-step prediction.
Its fulfillment during the recursive simulation is not mathematically intended.
Rather, it represents a pragmatic approach to approximate uncertainty propagation with one-step-ahead 
predictions.
To rigorously achieve the desired coverage along the complete horizon, mulit-step prediction models are needed.
\newline
\newline
Nonetheless, the tendency towards over-coverage is welcomed compared to under-coverage,
as it enforces contraint satisfaction and thus safety over objective performance.  
The introduction of an \emph{a posteriori}-only or naive
PC-activation function does not lead to recognizable differences to the basic NARX framework. 
In contrast, a PC activation function applied during training and inference
achieves a change. It improves the learning of equation governed data as shown (sec.~\ref{results:sec:training-efficiency}). 
In the recursive forward simulation though, it causes minimally wider confidence intervals and
smaller deviations in the coverage predictions. It is neither counted as a significant improvement, nor a
deterioration in the straight simulation.

\section{MPC Performance \& Robustness}
\label{MPC-Performance-and-Robustness}

As the last part of this work, the control performance of an MPC, that relies on the mentioned
NARX frameworks vanilla, naive and PC (pc) is to be analyzed.
On one hand, an apropriate MPC controller is postulated and tuned.
On the other hand, a series of closed-loop simulations is completed to calculate the
performance indicators selectivity, constraint violation, control effort and runtime.
To investigate the effect of the uncertainty quantification (UQ), the MPC is run including a
nominal NARX architecture with expected value models only.

\subsection{Optimization Formulation}
Following optimization formulation is proposed (eq.~\ref{results:eq:MPC-formulation-TR}).

\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:MPC-formulation-TR}
    \begin{gathered}
        \minimize{\vsy{t:t+N},\bm{T}_{\text{w},[t:t+N-1]}}\quad \quad \bigg ( \sum^{t+N-1}_{i=k}  -(S_{\text{EO},i})^2
        + \Delta \bm{T}_{\text{w},i}^\top \bm{R} \Delta \bm{T}_{\text{w},i} \bigg )
        - (S_{\text{EO},t+N})^2
        \\
        \begin{aligned}
            \text{s.t.} \quad  \bm{y}_{k+1} &= f_{\text{NARX}}(\bm{\phi}_k) \\
            \bm{T}_k &\leq T_\text{max} \\
            X &\geq X_\text{min} \\
            \bm{T}_{\text{w},k} &\leq \bm{T}_k \\
            T_{\text{w,lb}} &\leq \bm{T}_{\text{w},k} \leq T_{\text{w,ub}}
        \end{aligned}
    \end{gathered}
\end{equation}

\noindent
The cost of ethylene makes up roughly \SI{68}{\%} of the total costs \cite{pietschak2018}. Therefore, the
profitability is mainly determined by the selectivity of towards ethylene oxide $S_\text{EO}$.
Hence, an economic objective function is chosen to maximize selectivity, while a minimum required
conversion $X_\text{min}$ is to be achieved. To reduce catalyst degradation, the maximum temperature is required
to stay below $T_\text{max}$. 
Temperature and conversion are positively correlated, as high temperatures favor faster chemistry.
However, selectivity towards ethylene oxide and temperature are negatively correlated due to the 
ratio of true activation energies (eq.~\ref{results:eq:ratio-activation-energies}).
The total oxidation of ethylene accelerates faster with rising temperature.
\newline
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:ratio-activation-energies}
    \frac{E_\text{A,1}^{\text{(true)}}}{E_\text{A,2}^{\text{(true)}}} \approx 0.83
\end{equation}

\noindent
A ratio below $1.0$ demands low temperatures for a high selectivity towards the products of the first reaction
and vice versa.
Thus, a trade-off is introduced. The controller needs to find a compromise between high selectivity,
adequate conversion and maximum temperature. Additionally, the temperature decisive model parameters 
for reaction rate and heat conduction are subject to uncertainty and the throughput to fluctuation.
To realize faster optimization time, while considering the system's slowest dynamics at
the scale of \SI{20}{\second}, an MPC prediction time step of \SI{2}{\second} with a horizon length of 
$N = 13$ is proposed. This equates to a total prediction horizon of \SI{26}{\second} and
must be incorporated by the NARX models. Therefore, they are retrained on the same train and calibration
data sets with an adjusted prediction time step of  $\Delta t_\text{pred} = \SI{2}{\second}$.
Also, the control frequency
is set to $f = \SI{0.5}{\second^{-1}}$ which is equivalent to the length of the prediction time step.
The first principles model simulation remains at a time step of $\Delta t_\text{fp} = \SI{1}{\second}$.
All specifications for the MPC algorithm are listed (tab.~\ref{results:tab:MPC-configuration}).

\begin{table}[!h]
    \centering
    \caption[Configuration for the MPC algorithm.]

    \begin{tabular}{cccccccccccccc}

        $r_{44}$ \\
        $13$ & $1$ & \SI{2}{\second} & \SI{1}{\second} & \SI{0.5}{\second^{-1}} & $250$ & $20$ & $15$ &

        \midrule
        $T_\text{max}$ & $X_\text{min}$ & 
        $T_{\text{w,lb}}$ & $T_{\text{w,ub}}$ & $\lambda_1$ & $\lambda_2$ & $\lambda_3$ \\

    \label{results:tab:MPC-configuration}

\noindent
\newline
For the implementation of the NARX-based UQ MPC algorithm
with \texttt{do-mpc}, a robust horizon $N_\text{r}$ of one allows for an exact branching into 
one nominal, one upper and one lower scenario. 
To avoid input oscillation,
fluctuating wall temperatures are penalized using the diagonal matrix $\bm{R}$ with the coefficients $r_{ll}$ of 
$250$, $20$, $15$ and $10$. Here, the very first wall temperature has the biggest effect on the thermal behavior
of the TR. Due to the plug-flow it is most prone to induce oscillation into the system. Thus, its change
is penalized much higher than the other temperatures. The missing dispersion of the flow makes the system
especially sensitive to varying inputs. Further, the wall temperatures are subject to a lower bound $T_\text{w,lb}$ of 
\SI{580}{} and an upper bound $T_\text{w,ub}$ of \SI{625}{\kelvin}. 
The cooling is realized by water evaporation in the jacket and adjusted via the vapor pressure.
Therefore, only reactor cooling is possible and heating should be made infeasible for the controller.
To ensure negative heat flow, the
cooling temperatures of the reactor jacket $\bm{T}_{\text{w},k}$ must remain below the reaction temperature 
at the respective measurements $\bm{T}_k$. Otherwise, the controller would also exploit heating to 
further improve the selectivity which is not allowed.
A pitfall of this constraint is that it cannot be guaranteed at every exact axial position but only for the 
depicted measurements. It is introduced to provide a somewhat better realism and
it is not considered a crucial constraint for performance or robustness.
For better convergence of the interior point optimizer
\cite{Waechter2006}, the constraints on maximum temperature, minimum conversion and negative heat flow are
considered as \emph{soft} constraints. Their respective slack variables are given as $\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$ and
$\lambda_3$. A violation of the maximum temperature constraint as well as finessing the heat flow direction
is to be avoided with the highest penalty of $1000$. In contrast, the minimum conversion is a guiding reference
to limit a recycle stream to a reasonable amount. Thus, a small violation can be allowed to help meeting
the more critical constraints or avoid temperature oscillation under difficult operating conditions.
Most importantly, the system propagation is executed by the respective NARX architecture for uncertainty
quantification $f_\text{NARX}$ as a function of past measurements and inputs $\bm{\phi}_k$. 
All mentioned parameters for the MPC algorithm are found during manual tuning to find a good balance
between selectivity, conversion, maximum temperature violation, input oscillation and runtime.
While other tuning approaches such as plain grid search or Bayesian optimization exist,
the manual procedure allows for a better understanding of the MPC-system relationship.


\subsection{Nominal NARX}

To control the system, one time-varying signal is sampled for the input flow speed as well as 
a constant combination for the first principle model parameters.
While the inlet temperature of \SI{625}{\kelvin} and mole fractions are constant, the inlet
flow speed $u$ can fluctuate in steep ramps between \SI{0.2}{} and \SI{0.4}{\meter \; \second^{-1}}.
Using \SI{0.4}{\meter \; \second^{-1}} as the reference, the reactor is confronted with a load 
range between \SI{100}{\%} and \SI{50}{\%} (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-nominal} (c)).
This is assumed to be challenging and realistic at the same time like in
carbon dioxide methanation \cite{fischer2021}.
Other than the uncertain parameters, the flow speed is known in advance by the MPC algorithm and
implemented as a time-varying parameter (\texttt{tvp}) in \texttt{do-mpc}. 
A control loop is executed over $200$ control steps with given control step length of \SI{2}{\second} 
and simulation step length of \SI{1}{\second} (tab.~\ref{results:tab:MPC-configuration}).
With a duration of \SI{20}{} times the system's greatest time constant, this horizon length is considered 
representative for a real application.
The nominal NARX architecture employs expected value NNs only and does not account for
UQ (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-nominal}).
The color gradient denotes the measurement position along the $z$-coordinate.
Purple represents $z/L = 0.25$, blue $0.50$, green $0.75$ and yellow $1.0$, which is effectively the outlet. 
Further, the graphic shows the reactor temperature as
most important system measurement (a), the control
inputs (b), the inlet flow speed (c), conversion and selectivity (d) (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-nominal}).
\newline

To have a reference, the nominal result is discussed briefly. 
A more detailed explaination of the control is done for the 
more rigorous vanilla UQ-based MPC (sec.~\ref{results:sec:vanilla-NARX}).
The nominal MPC forecasts violations of the minimum conversion constraint and increases the first wall temperature
at \SI{60}{}, \SI{160}{}, \SI{240}{} and \SI{320}{\second} (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-nominal}~(b)).
This increase occurs harshly to values
above \SI{605}{\kelvin}.
Consequently, the inputs suffer a lot from undershoots and oscillation in all cooling compartments

Due to the uncaptured plant-model mismatch, the nominal MPC repeatedly exceeds the critical maximum

temperature constraint, underlines how poorly the surrogates describe the parametric uncertainty.
Eventhough maximum temperature exceedings are performed, which endager safe operation,
the nominal MPC does not avoid violations of minimum conversion
(fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-nominal}~(d)~III).

\subsection{Vanilla NARX}
\label{results:sec:vanilla-NARX}

An MPC is run with the vanilla surrogate ensemble based on UQ via CQR with the
exact same parameter combination and inlet flow speed trajectory as the nominal one.
The resulting trajectories for the temperature measurements, inputs, throughput flow rate,
selectivity and conversion are plotted (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla}).
To have a better idea about the system as a whole, the dimensionless concentration
measures $\chi_{(\cdot)}$ are indicated as well.
\newline

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Figures/Results/MPC/control_loop_vanilla.pdf}
    \caption[Control loop - vanilla NARX framework.]
    {Control loop with the vanilla NARX framework. (a-e) dimensionless concentration measures for
    all components. (f) reactor temperatures. (g) wall cooling temperatures. (h) inlet flow speed.
    (i) selectivity and conversion. (j) optimization duration per step. Axial coordinate as color gradient
    from purple to yellow.}
    \label{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Regarding the dimensionless concentration measures $\chi_{(\cdot)}$ for the components
(fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (a–e)), 
a reasonable consumption of educts and production of products is indicated.
Ethylene (E) and oxygen (O$_2$) decrease over the reactor length with $z/L = 0.25$ (purple)
showing the highest and the reactor outlet (yellow) the lowest values.
Vice versa, the formed species ethylene oxide (EO), water (H$_2$O) and carbon dioxide (CO$_2$)
increase.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
It is to be analysed how the MPC reacts to fluctuating input flow speeds in terms of
the wall temperature (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (g-h)).
It is visible that the controller increases the cooling temperature for rising flow speeds.
To meet the specified minimum conversion $X_\text{min}$ ((i), black, dash-dotted),
the reaction rate must be accelerated by lesser cooling.
Higher flow speeds decrease the residence time in such a way that the minimum conversion constraint
becomes violated if the temperature is not risen.
This happens in the range between \SI{0}{} and \SI{40}{\second}, when the flow rapidly enlarges
to \SI{0.4}{\meter \; \second^{-1}} and the conversion (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (i) III)
hits its lower boundary of $0.5$. Here, the MPC with the vanilla NARX framework is able 
to anticipate the constraint violation and counteract with reduced cooling in all compartments
(fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (g)). The same behavior is observed at \SI{160}{}, \SI{260}{}
and \SI{320}{\second}, where the throughput amplifies each time.
Increases in the first cooling temperature occur more gentle with a maximum
of \SI{600}{\kelvin} compared to the nominal MPC
with values up to \SI{605}{\kelvin} (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-nominal}~(b)).
Additionally, the controller prefers to cool the reaction mixture at the beginning of the reactor (purple)
more strongly to counteract a hotspot formation (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (g)).
To correct for unreacted educts, the wall temperatures
along the remaining reactor are set to higher levels (blue, green, yellow). The fact, that
the latter three cooling compartments show mostly equal cooling temperatures indicate that they do not 
influence the reaction as much as the first cooling temperature. 
Therefore, it is valid to penalize variations in the first cooling temperature the most.
For a real construction, it is more
suitable to introduce smaller cooling compartments where a hotspot is probable to form.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
Especially after the initialization and jumps in the input flow speed,
the cooling temperatures at $z/L = 0.75$ (green) and 
at the outlet (yellow) (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (g-h) I) oscillate.
This occurs at the time instances \SI{0}{}, \SI{80}{}, \SI{180}{}, \SI{260}{} and \SI{340}{\second}
and becomes likely for two reasons. First, the latter two wall temperatures own the smaller
regularization terms of $r_{33} = 15$ and $r_{44} = 10$.
Second, the reactor is confronted with a drastic and abrupt change in load.
The observed oscillations appear to follow the load increases at 
\SI{0}{}, \SI{80}{}, \SI{160}{}, \SI{250}{} and \SI{320}{\second}
(fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (h)).
Here, the controller must react to the lower residence time and the threat to cross the conversion
constraint. 
The regularization of the control inputs becomes smaller compared to the soft constraint
violation and vanish from the objective function landscape.
Thus, the MPC chooses to change the input more often.
These oscillations, that follow an undershoot,
are undesired (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla}~(g)~(green) I)
and indicate how badly the convection dominated system damps disturbances.
Because the lack of axial dispersion does not allow for natural damping, the controller
must be able to reliably predict the system dynamics to avoid oscillation.
Here, it must be assumed that numerical diffusion due to spatial discretization is the only contribution 
to damping.
As it only acts on the level of the truncation error of
$\mathcal{O}(\Delta z)$ of the backward finite difference method, its effect is extremely weak.
In case of the PFTR, numerical diffusion contributes to axial dispersion, eventhough 
dispersion and diffusion are separate phenomena in a physical sense.
For better stability, it is more suitable and at the same time realistic to model axial dispersion of the flow.
Besides a high prediction accuracy, a long-enough prediction horizon and a greater regularization term
could reduce switching of the input. Further, it is interesting, whether a PC-NARX
framework is able to yield an improvement or not.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
Moving on to the reactor temperature (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla}~(f)),
the controller is able to avoid the violation of the maximum temperature constraint (II, black, dash-dotted)
of \SI{630}{\kelvin} for all temperature measurements $T$.
Especially at the outlet of the reactor (yellow), the controller operates close to the constraint
without a violation. 
In contrast to the nominal MPC, the vanilla MPC with UQ
can maneuver the reactor very closely at the maximum temperature
(fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (f)) in robust manner.
While undershoots and oscillations appear,
they are reduced compared to the nominal MPC. This comes not at the cost of a 
lower conversion (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (i) III). Nominal and vanilla
MPC controllers achieve comparable violations of the conversion limit. So, the employment 
of quantile regression NARX models causes a significant improvement regarding robustness in
the temperature (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla} (f) II).
This is achieved eventhough the kinetic
parameters of the first principle model are not only unknown but generated randomly.
The UQ via quantile regression works and ensures constraint satisfaction
for the uncertain measurement of the temperature for this specific parameter combination.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
Nonetheless, the controller accepts violation of the minimum conversion at \SI{30}{}, \SI{100}{}
and \SI{360}{\second}, because a further temperature increase would risk the more critical temperature
constraint violation (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla}~(i)~III). Compared to the conversion, the selectivity
(purple) remains stagnating at $0.81$ and shows very little sensitivity towards the temperature.
This is simply caused by the low temperature regime. To have a significant change in selectivity, higher
temperatures are required. Therefore, the controller mainly focuses on balancing the trade-off between
conversion and temperature for high flow speeds. For low flow speeds, minimum conversion can be achieved easily.
That is why, the controller focuses on optimizing the selectivity which requires lower temperatures 
simultaneously.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
Lastly, the runtimes of each recursive optimization in \texttt{ipopt} \cite{Waechter2006} is presented
(fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla}~(j)).,
These vary between \SI{2}{} and \SI{10}{\second} after the inital optimization run, which takes
\SI{90}{\second}. It is ususal in recursive optimization for the initial step to take more 
time, because the initial guess of the decision variables is not close to the optimal solution
even if it set accordingly in \texttt{do-mpc}. The solution time shows a slight correlation
with the temperature in the first cooling section (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-vanilla}~(g)~(purple)).
Every time the MPC suggests a higher value than its lower bound of \SI{580}{\kelvin}, the
solution duration climbs to approximately \SI{6}{\second}. This is likely a result of a more complex
optimization landscape. Here, the system is endangered to hit the minimum conversion constraint
due to a higher input flow. Thus, the penalty of the slack variable $\lambda_2$ induces
a gradient, that pushes the interior point away from the strict inequality constraint of the 
input variable. Additionally, the control input regularization becomes less influential,
that previously may caused a local minimum. The optimizer needs to find a new one leading
to an increased convergence duration.
With regard to real applicability, the \texttt{ipopt} solution time
becomes an obstacle for the control frequency of \SI{0.5}{\second^{-1}}. 
To realize feasibility, the time step length of the prediction horizon may be doubled to \SI{4}{\second}
which would effectively reduce the amount of optimization variables to one half.
At best, the computational effort for quadratic problems (QP) with a sparse matrix structure such as 
this MPC formulation grows linearly with the prediction horizon $\mathcal{O}(N)$ \cite{stark2025}.
Therefore, the solution time would shrink roughly by \SI{50}{\%}.
Further, the training of the NARX models must be adjusted accordingly
to account for a \SI{4}{\second} time step. This would come at the cost of a worse
representation of the fast system dynamics.
Another solution can be approximate MPC, where the learning-based controller itself is learned
to replace the expensive second-order optimization.




\subsection{Naive NARX}

With the naively PC-NARX framework, the MPC receives predictions that align 
with the conservation of elements. At first sight, the vanilla and
the naive MPC algorithms do not differ noticably (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-naive}).
Neither inputs (b), nor conversion (d) show a significant difference. This is suggested
by the previously done simulation capability (sec.~\ref{results:sec:simulation-capability}) and
uncertainty quantification (sec.~\ref{results:sec:uncertain-quantification}) experiments, 
where the naive application of the PC activation did not alter
the outcome remarkably.
\newline 

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\textwidth]{Figures/Results/MPC/control_loop_naive.pdf}
    \caption[Control loop - naive NARX framework.]

\subsection{Overall Comparison}
\label{sec:overall-comparison}

To have empirical evidence about the differences in the MPC algorithms based on the four
NARX ensembles, multiple control loops for varying parameter combinations are conducted. 
The inlet flow speed trajectory remains (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-loop-pc}~(c)). This is 
done for $16$ distinct parameter combinations, that are sampled in MC fashion.
For measuring the quality of the control, four key performance indicators (KPIs) are 
considered. These include the mean selectivity, that is described as the mean selectivity
of ethylene towards ethylene oxide over time and all trajectories (eq.~\ref{results:eq:mean-selectivity}).
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:mean-selectivity}
    \bar{S}_\text{EO} \coloneq \langle S_\text{EO} \rangle_{i, j}
\end{equation}
Where $j$ equates to the index of the control run and $i$ to the discrete time instant.
Further, constraint violation (vio) is measured for the respective variables temperature and conversion
separately as the fraction of points, which lie in the infeasible region (eq.~\ref{results:eq:temperature-violation}). 
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:temperature-violation}
    T_\text{vio} \coloneq \langle \bm{1}_{T_{j,i,k} > T_\text{max}} \rangle_{k, i, j}
\end{equation}
The number of violating points is counted via the indicator function $\bm{1}$ and averaged 
over the measurement position $k$, time $i$ and trajectory $j$. For the minimum conversion $X_\text{vio}$,
this is done analogously. To have a combined indicator, that also includes the criticality
of both constraints, a weighted violation $C_\text{vio}$ is calculated.
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:combined-violation}
    C_\text{vio} \coloneq \lambda_1 T_\text{vio} + \lambda_2 X_\text{vio}
\end{equation}
This is achieved by linear combining both violation indicators with their respective
soft constraint
regularization variables $\lambda_1$ and $\lambda_2$ (tab.~\ref{results:tab:MPC-configuration}).
Oscillation or even chattering of the input signals is undesired, as they 
make reaching a stable operating point more difficult. As well, they increase mechanical wear
of actuators. 
Therefore, the control
effort $J_{\Delta T_\text{w}}$ measures all temporal changes in the control input. It accounts
for rapid switches as well as gradual input changes that might be unnecessary (eq.~\ref{results:eq:control-effort}).
\begin{equation}
    \label{results:eq:control-effort}
    J_{\Delta T_\text{w}} \coloneq \langle (\bm{T}_{\text{w},i,j} - \bm{T}_{\text{w},i-1,j})^\top
    \bm{R} (\bm{T}_{\text{w},i,j} - \bm{T}_{\text{w},i-1,j}) \rangle_{i, j}
\end{equation}
In contrast to the objective function in the MPC formulation (eq.~\ref{results:eq:MPC-formulation-TR}),
the wall temperatures appear without scaling. Thus, the unit of the control effort equates to $\text{K}^2$.
Lastly, the average solution time of the QP in \texttt{ipopt} is calculated as $\Delta t_\text{ipopt}$.
All KPIs are listed for the four MPC variations nominal, vanilla, naive and PC
(tab.~\ref{results:tab:MPC-KPIs}). Additionally, the four most important system variables
(outlet temperature, first cooling temperature, selectivity and conversion) are plotted for
all $16$ runs (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-comparison}) to clarify the KPIs with the actual system 
trajectories.

Apparently, the temperature constraint violation is at zero for all
NARX ensembles with UQ. The quantile NARX models, that are based
on conformalized quantile regression, are able to achieve robustness. 
Eventhough they are calibrated for the \SI{90}{\%}-quantile, they do not allow for \SI{10}{\%}
of violation. This aligns with the finding of strict over-coverage during 
the assessment of uncertainty quantification (sec.~\ref{results:sec:uncertain-quantification}).
Especially, the last temperature measurement is most prone to exceed the limit, where
the biggest over-coverage is observed (fig.~\ref{results:fig:naive-coverage-width}).
This is further illustrated by the outlet temperature trajectories, which 
remain precisely below the maximimum constraint for all runs 
(fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-comparison}~(a), I, yellow) with UQ. 
In contrast, the nominal MPC crosses the maximimum boundary repeatedly for a large number of runs.
Thus, quantile regression represents an advantage over the nominal NARX architecture, 
which exhibits a significant amount of temperature constraint violation of \SI{11.2}{\%}.
W.r.t conversion, which is not treated as a robust constraint, all MPCs with UQ 
cause greater violations than the nominal one. This is due to the robustness regarding
the maximum temperature. UQ-based MPCs can anticipate temperature exceedings and
therefore prefer risking violations of minimum conversion.
Because of the trade-off between temperature and 
conversion, the nominal controller simply performs better with \SI{15.9}{\%} violation than the
UQ-based controllers by exploiting vorbidden temperatures. The vanilla- and naive-based 
MPCs show equal violations with \SI{29.0}{\%} which again indicates that the naive application of the 
pc activation function leaves negligible impact. Here, the PC-based MPC achieves a better
value of \SI{24.3}{\%}. When considering the combined constraint violation $C_\text{vio}$, 
the nominal MPC performs worst with a value of \SI{0.113}{} followed by the vanilla and naive
with identical values of \SI{5.69e-3}{} and the PC version with \SI{4.77e-3}{} of violation.
In this case, the MPCs with uncertainty awareness using CQR perform at least \SI{95.0}{\%} better than 
the nominal MPC in constraint violation. When physics-constraints are added and used during NARX
training, the robustness increases by \SI{95.8}{\%} (tab.~\ref{results:tab:MPC-improvement-percentage}).

\noindent
The control effort $J_{\Delta T_\text{w}}$ for the MPCs with UQ decreases by \SI{39.2}{\%} from \SI{829}{\kelvin^2}
to \SI{504}{\kelvin^2} for the vanilla and naive frameworks. The addition of a pc
activation allows for a \SI{44.4}{\%} decrease to \SI{461}{\kelvin^2}.
This is due to the reduced oscillations and more gentle inputs visible in the 
cooling temperature trajectories (fig.~\ref{results:fig:MPC-comparison}~(a)~purple, II and III).
On one hand, the UQ is able to capture worst-case temperature scenarios and thus prevent harsh
inputs that need to be corrected later which leads to oscillatory behavior. On the other hand,
the PC framework yields lower input oscillations. Here, it can be assumed that the application
of the PC activation reduces the recursive accumulation of predictions errors within the MPC
horizon. In turn, the optimization landscape becomes smoother and less noisy. 
Consequently, the optimizer converges to local minima faster and more precise
leading to less variation in optimal solutions for the input.
This assumption is backed by a shorter mean solution duration of the PC-based MPC of \SI{3.80}{\second}
compared to \SI{4.78}{} and \SI{4.80}{\second} of naive and vanilla, that are almost identical.
As the naive application does not exhibit this improvement, it must be caused by the training process.
Here, the correction of NN outputs in forward and backward passes facilitates the learning
such that the validation loss shrinks faster (fig.~\ref{results:fig:validation_loss}).
The correcting effect on the data within one batch surpresses noise in the gradient which
is especially beneficial in stochastic gradient descent. Thus, the parameter updates and the 
network parameters themselves are less noisy. During the recursive forward simulation
(sec.~\ref{results:sec:simulation-capability}), this does not lead to a significant change,
because it does not require a network gradient.
But for neural MPC, NNs are recursively applied as a function combination through the prediction
horizon and differentiated thereafter.
Thus, the improved network gradient enables smoother and faster convergence which is observable 
in reduced oscillations and faster solution times.
\newline
\newline
\noindent
The greater network complexity caused by introducing the PC activation function does not worsen
optimization time. Here, the frameworks naive and vanilla do not show a significant difference.
In fact, the reduction of prediction errors outweighs the greater complexity in terms of
solution time. It is important to mention, that the application of additional quantile NARX models leads 
to an increase in solution time by \SI{151.7}{\%} when compared to the PC-NARX architecture.
